Mr. Sunshine (미스터 선샤인) is set at the end of the Joseon dynasty as Japanese
colonial expansion takes hold, following characters drawn into the early
independence movement. It's a big-budget, openly patriotic historical drama,
and this week treats that as exactly the thing to examine: historical dramas
don't just depict the past, they construct a version of national memory that
audiences carry forward as if it were simply what happened.

## Questions

- How is the late Joseon period and Japanese colonial expansion represented in
  the drama?
- What forms did Korean resistance actually take, and which of those does the
  drama show?
- What does the drama remember, simplify, or romanticise about this period?
- How does historical drama, as a genre, construct national memory --- and what
  responsibility does that carry?

## What to take from this week

- the late Korean Empire and the conditions that preceded annexation
- Japanese colonialism and its scope, beyond what a single drama can depict
- independence movements, in their actual variety --- armed, diplomatic,
  cultural
- resistance as organised, sustained political action, not a single hero's
  arc
- national identity as something this period is still cited to explain
- historical memory, and the gap between a national story and the historical
  record

## Approaching this week

Be historically responsible here. Treat the drama's characters and events as
fiction inspired by the period, not as documentary evidence --- a compelling
scene is not a source, and a claim about the colonial period needs a historical
source behind it, not just a citation of the show.
