KPop Demon Hunters (케이팝 데몬 헌터스) folds a girl-group act into a monster-hunting
premise, and the seam between the two --- polished performance and genre
plot --- is exactly where this week lives. K-pop is the most globally visible
part of contemporary Korean culture, and this week asks what that visibility
is actually made of: a production system, a fandom, and a set of choices about
what of "Korea" gets exported and what doesn't.

## Questions

- Why has K-pop become globally influential, when so much national pop music
  doesn't travel?
- How does Korean popular culture combine local identity with globally
  engineered production?
- What does K-pop reveal about contemporary Korean identity, as opposed to
  what it's marketed as revealing?
- Where do entertainment, fandom, technology, and cultural export actually
  intersect --- and where are they just adjacent?

## What to take from this week

- K-pop as an industrial system (training, production, choreography) rather
  than a genre of song
- fandom as an organised, often transnational, labour of its own
- Hallyu / the Korean Wave, and what "wave" implies about direction and export
- cultural export as policy as much as taste
- globalisation's effect on what gets made, not just on who watches it
- Korean popular culture beyond K-pop, and what K-pop's visibility crowds out
