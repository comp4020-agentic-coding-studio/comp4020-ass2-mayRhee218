The 1st Shop of Coffee Prince (커피프린스 1호점) is built on a premise common to
the genre: a woman disguised as a man, and the confusion --- comic and
otherwise --- that follows. Watched now, it's as much a record of what a 2007
Korean audience found thinkable, sayable, and safely resolvable as it is a
romance. This week asks what the disguise premise needed to be true about its
audience in order to work as entertainment, and how much of that has moved on.

## Questions

- How did Korean television represent gender and sexuality in the 2000s?
- What does the show's gender-disguise premise reveal about the social
  expectations it was written against?
- How have Korean attitudes and media representations changed since?
- What is the difference between a media representation and a lived
  experience --- and why does collapsing the two produce bad arguments?

## What to take from this week

- how Korean television represented gender and sexuality in the 2000s, and
  what counted as a safe way to raise the subject
- what a gender-disguise plot needs its audience to find both plausible and
  resolvable
- change over time in Korean attitudes and their media representation, not a
  single fixed "Korean attitude"
- the gap between representation and lived experience, and why that gap
  matters to how we argue from a drama

## Approaching this week

This topic needs handling carefully and academically. Avoid making simplistic
claims about "what Koreans think" --- Korean society, like any other, holds a
range of views that have also changed considerably since 2007. The discussion
is about what the drama assumed and represented, not a verdict on a whole
society's beliefs.
